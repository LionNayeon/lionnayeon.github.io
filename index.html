01 / Biography

About the researcher

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Consumer Economics at the University of Georgia. My research focuses on responsible AI, consumer well-being, and algorithmic decision-making, with particular interest in how consumers assess competing AI values and the trade-offs among them.

Alongside my doctoral studies, I am pursuing an M.S. in Data Science at the University of Georgia. I earned an M.S. in Consumer Science from Incheon National University, where I graduated with the Graduate Dean’s Award for Excellence (GPA: 4.50/4.50). I also earned a B.S. in Consumer Science and a B.B.A. from the same university with Highest Honors (GPA: 4.33/4.50).

I am a Research Assistant with Consumer Reports, contributing to participatory research, outreach, and statistical analyses of AI-enabled pricing practices. I also serve as an Instructor for FHCE 2100E: Introduction to Consumer Economics at the University of Georgia.

02 / Research Interests

Areas of inquiry

Research at the intersection of consumer economics, responsible AI, and algorithmic decision-making.

01

Responsible AI

How principles of responsible AI relate to consumer preferences and decision-making.

02

Algorithmic Fairness

Public preferences and trade-offs between predictive performance and fairness in AI decisions.

03

Consumer Well-Being

How technology-mediated consumer experiences relate to decision-making, autonomy, and well-being.

04

AI-Enabled Financial Advice

Psychological factors underlying consumers’ reliance on AI financial advice.

05

Algorithmic Pricing

AI-enabled pricing practices and their implications for price disparities and fairness concerns.

06

AI-Enabled Housing

Consumer autonomy and perceived allocation of agency in AI-enabled housing environments.

Research approach

My research employs choice-based conjoint analysis, survey experiments, regression and mediation analyses, and qualitative and computational content analysis. Through these approaches, I examine how consumers evaluate AI systems, weigh competing values, and make trade-offs in their decision-making.

05 / Research Impact

AI-driven pricing: research & public impact

Evidence, public coverage, and policy attention surrounding Consumer Reports' investigation into Instacart's AI-enabled pricing experiments.

My contribution: Statistical analysisDEC 2025

Same cart. Different prices.

As a Consumer Reports research assistant, I contributed statistical analysis to the joint investigation with Groundwork Collaborative and More Perfect Union. The study documented price differences for identical grocery items and shopping baskets shown to different shoppers at the same time. The Consumer Reports article credits my statistical analysis.

Regulatory attention

Calls for FTC scrutiny

U.S. senators requested an FTC investigation into Instacart's pricing following the public research. This was a request for review, not an FTC enforcement finding about the experiments.

Senators' FTC letter (Dec 2025) FTC personalized-pricing policy (2026)

The 2026 FTC proposal is broader policy context, not a claimed direct outcome of this project.

Company response

Instacart ends price tests

On December 22, 2025, Instacart announced it would discontinue its item-level price experiments on the platform after the investigation and public criticism.

Consumer Reports announcement Reuters coverage
State policy impact · Maryland · 2026

Maryland's Protection from Predatory Pricing Act (HB 895)

In testimony submitted to Maryland lawmakers in February 2026, Consumer Reports cited the joint Instacart investigation, including its findings on AI-enabled grocery price experiments, and recommended extending the legislation to third-party grocery delivery platforms. Maryland subsequently enacted HB 895 (Chapter 154), effective October 1, 2026, addressing certain dynamic-pricing and consumer-data pricing practices.

Maryland HB 895 · Official legislation Consumer Reports legislative testimony (PDF) Consumer Reports response to the enacted law

This shows a documented use of the investigation in policy advocacy, not proof that the investigation itself caused the law to pass. Consumer Reports also expressed concern that amendments left loopholes and weak enforcement in the final law.

The investigation and follow-up developments are reported by the linked organizations. The study concerns randomized item-price experiments; the broader debate over personalized or surveillance pricing is distinct.
